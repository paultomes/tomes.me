Ownership in the Age of AI

On intelligence becoming abundant, what may remain scarce, and why enjoying life still matters more than winning it.

Written by Paul Tomes · 21 July 2026

To my dearest Adam, Daniel, Jack and Phoebe,

I have been thinking a lot about the world in which you will spend most of your lives.

By the time you read this again in twenty or thirty years, some of what I write will probably seem obvious. Some of it may seem hopelessly wrong. That is fine. This is not intended to be a set of instructions or a prediction of the future.

It is simply a record of how I was thinking in 2026, at the age of fifty-three, when artificial intelligence had started to become genuinely useful and the pace of change appeared to be accelerating.

My hope is not that you adopt all my conclusions. It is that you think carefully about the questions.

Enjoy your life

Before talking about artificial intelligence, ownership, money or investment, I want to start with something more important.

Enjoy your lives.

Travel. Laugh. Spend time with people you love. Try things that make you uncomfortable. Have hobbies. Go to parties. Read books. Play sport. Make mistakes. Learn to cook. Sit around a table for too long with good friends.

Do not become so focused on preparing for the future that you forget to experience the present.

This is one reason I have always valued university for more than the qualification. A degree may help open a door, but the larger value often lies in leaving home, meeting people from different backgrounds, developing independence and building friendships that may last for the rest of your life.

The social networks you build are not merely contacts or career opportunities. They are part of the structure of a good life.

A life that looks successful from the outside but is not enjoyable from the inside is not success.

Intelligence is becoming abundant

For much of human history, intelligence, education and specialist knowledge were scarce.

People could build valuable careers because they knew how to write, calculate, design, analyse, code, negotiate, diagnose or explain things that most other people could not.

Artificial intelligence is beginning to change that.

I can already complete work in minutes that previously took hours. I can explore ideas, analyse documents, write software, draft proposals and consider alternative arguments much faster than I could before.

The capability remains imperfect, but it will not remain at today's level. It will become faster, cheaper, more capable and more deeply embedded in everyday life.

This does not mean human intelligence will become worthless. It means that intelligence alone may become less scarce.

When something becomes abundant, the value tends to move elsewhere.

What remains scarce?

If knowledge and cognitive labour become cheaper, we should ask what cannot be produced infinitely at almost no cost.

Land remains limited. Energy requires infrastructure. Houses occupy physical space. Food must be grown and transported. Factories, logistics networks, machines and robots must exist somewhere in the real world.

Trust remains scarce. Reputation remains scarce. Good judgement remains scarce. Courage remains scarce. The ability to take responsibility remains scarce.

Human relationships remain scarce because they require shared history, attention and genuine care.

And ownership remains scarce.

A question worth asking

What will still be difficult to create, difficult to copy, limited in supply or essential to human life when intelligence is cheap and widely available?

Learn what value means

Money is often confused with value, but they are not the same thing.

Money is a way of measuring and exchanging value. Real value comes from doing something useful for another person.

A farmer creates value by producing food. A builder creates value by providing shelter. A doctor creates value by improving health. A musician may create value through happiness or emotion. A business creates value when it solves a problem for its customers.

You should regularly ask:

What problem am I solving, who am I solving it for, and why does it matter to them?

The world generally rewards people who create genuine value, although not always immediately and not always fairly.

Do not simply ask what job pays the most. Ask where value is likely to be created in the future and how you can participate in creating it.

Ownership matters

Most people begin their working lives by exchanging time for money. There is nothing wrong with this. Work provides experience, discipline, independence, friendships and purpose.

But time is finite.

No matter how talented you are, there are only so many hours you can sell. Artificial intelligence may make those hours more productive, but it may also reduce the price people are willing to pay for many forms of cognitive work.

Ownership works differently.

A share in a productive company can continue creating value without requiring another hour of your time. A business can serve thousands or millions of people. A piece of infrastructure can remain useful for decades. Intellectual property can be used repeatedly. Energy assets can continue generating power.

This is why I want you to understand the difference between earning and owning.

Earning provides income.

Ownership gives you participation in future value.

Where possible, try to become an owner as well as a worker.

Own shares in businesses. Perhaps build a business of your own. Invest consistently. Understand property, land, energy, infrastructure and productive assets. Create things that retain value beyond the time taken to produce them.

Ownership is not only about becoming wealthy. It gives you agency. It gives you choices. It reduces your dependence on a single employer, government or economic system.

Do not confuse ownership with consumption

Build a life you enjoy. Own more than you consume. Stay curious.

Buying something does not automatically make it a valuable asset.

A more expensive car may be enjoyable, but it is usually a consuming asset. A house can be both a home and an asset, but not every house is a good investment. Land can be valuable, but location, regulation and use matter enormously.

A physical product is not valuable merely because it is physical. It must still be useful, desirable, difficult to replace or capable of producing something people need.

Always ask whether something consumes value, stores value or produces value.

There is nothing wrong with buying things for enjoyment. Life is not a spreadsheet. Just be honest with yourself about what you are buying and why.

Use AI, but keep your judgement

I do not think you should fear artificial intelligence or try to compete with it by doing the same work more slowly.

Learn to use it.

Use it to amplify your curiosity, productivity and creativity. Let it help you explore possibilities, learn new subjects and test your ideas.

But do not outsource your judgement.

AI can produce an answer without understanding the consequences. It can sound certain while being wrong. It does not carry the responsibility for what happens next.

You do.

The people who remain valuable may not be those who can produce the most information. They may be those who can decide which information matters, recognise when something is wrong and take responsibility for the result.

Build a reputation

In a world flooded with generated content, automated communication and synthetic identities, knowing who to trust may become more important than ever.

Your reputation is therefore an asset.

Do what you say you will do. Tell the truth, especially when it is uncomfortable. Admit mistakes quickly. Treat people well. Be useful. Do not take credit for other people's work. Do not behave differently towards someone simply because you think they have less power.

A good reputation takes years to build and moments to damage.

Unlike money, it cannot simply be inherited or purchased.

Invest early and patiently

One of the greatest advantages you have is time.

Invest from an early age, even if the amounts initially seem small. Learn how companies make money. Understand risk. Avoid putting everything into one idea, one company or one type of asset.

Do not spend your lives trying to predict what markets will do next week.

Think instead about what people are likely to need for decades: energy, shelter, food, healthcare, transport, communication, technology and trusted services.

Invest in productive assets and allow time to do the heavy lifting.

Compounding is rarely exciting at the beginning. Its power becomes visible much later.

Keep your independence of thought

Do not adopt an idea merely because it is fashionable, popular or repeated confidently.

That includes my ideas.

Listen carefully. Look at evidence. Consider incentives. Ask who benefits. Try to understand the strongest argument against your own position.

Change your mind when the facts change.

Intelligence is useful, but intellectual honesty is more important.

Wealth is not the final objective

Ownership and investment matter because they can provide freedom and resilience. They can allow you to choose how to spend your time, look after your family and help other people.

But accumulating assets without enjoying life is another form of failure.

Money cannot replace friendship, health, love, purpose or time with the people who matter.

Use wealth to support a good life. Do not allow the pursuit of wealth to consume it.

A final thought

I do not know exactly how artificial intelligence will change the world.

Perhaps work will become more enjoyable and productive. Perhaps people will have more freedom. Perhaps wealth will become more widely distributed.

Or perhaps ownership of technology, land, energy and infrastructure will concentrate economic power in fewer hands.

Most likely, the reality will be a complicated mixture of progress, disruption, opportunity and unfairness.

You cannot control the whole system.

You can remain curious. You can learn to create value. You can own a sensible share of productive assets. You can build relationships and a reputation. You can use new technology without allowing it to use you.

And throughout all of it, you can remember to enjoy being alive.

Love,

Dad

Context:

I wrote this on 21 July 2026, aged 53, while living in Winchester and running PassKit. AI tools had recently begun to make a meaningful difference to my daily work. Adam was 23, Daniel 20, Jack 18 and Phoebe 16. I could already see that intelligence and time were becoming less significant constraints, but nobody yet knew how far or how quickly the change would go.
