The Inner Game of Life

A reflection on performance, learning and enjoyment, and the art of being present in everyday life.

Written by Paul Tomes · 27 November 2026

I’ve just finished reading The Inner Game of Golf by Timothy Gallwey. I started reading it because I wanted to become a better golfer, but I’ve come away thinking far more about life than golf.

In fact, it’s made me question why I want to become a better golfer in the first place.

One of the concepts I found particularly fascinating is the idea of two selves, which Gallwey refers to as Self 1 and Self 2.

Self 1 is that seemingly endless voice in our heads. The one that tells us what to do, judges how well we’ve done it, worries about what might happen next and reminds us of what went wrong last time.

Self 2 is our more natural self. The part of us that experiences, learns, feels, responds and performs, often remarkably well when we allow it to do so without interference.

Think about catching a ball. We don’t consciously calculate its trajectory, the speed at which it’s travelling or the precise movements our muscles need to make. We simply catch it. Our natural abilities are quite extraordinary when we trust them.

Yet when we really care about the outcome of something, we often start interfering with those very abilities.

Golf is a brilliant example. The more I want to hit a good shot, the more instructions I give myself. And quite often, the worse the shot becomes.

But what has really got me thinking is how much this applies to almost everything else in life.

Why am I really doing this?

Gallwey explores three aspects of an activity: performance, learning and enjoyment.

It’s made me reflect on the relative importance I give to each of these, and whether, over the years, I’ve become a little too focused on performance.

Building a business, improving at something, solving a problem, achieving a goal. Even many of the things I do for pleasure have an element of wanting to get better at them.

And there’s nothing inherently wrong with that. I genuinely enjoy achieving things, learning new skills and challenging myself. I wouldn’t want to lose that part of who I am.

But I wonder how often my focus on the outcome detracts from the experience itself.

Am I playing golf to reduce my handicap or to enjoy playing golf?

Am I playing the guitar to become a better musician or to experience the pleasure of making music?

Am I exercising because I enjoy it or because I know it’s good for me?

Am I reading a book because I’m curious about something or because I believe reading it will somehow make me a better person?

Perhaps the more interesting question is why I want to get better in the first place.

Is it for my own satisfaction? Is it to impress someone else? Is it to live up to some expectation I’ve created for myself?

Or am I simply trying to impress Self 1?

Even when nobody else is watching, there can be an audience of one inside my head, assessing whether what I’m doing is good enough.

I wonder how much of what I do is influenced by my desire to satisfy that internal audience.

And I’m not necessarily suggesting there’s anything wrong with wanting to impress someone, including myself. I’m just finding it interesting to question my motivations a little more than I have done previously.

When life was simply play

Thinking back to my childhood, I remember playing on the beach in Bournemouth.

I don’t remember giving much thought to why I was doing it, what benefit I was getting from it or what I hoped to achieve by the end of the day.

I was simply playing.

Although, thinking about it, there was probably an element of performance in that too. There were things I wanted to do, things I wanted to discover and challenges I set myself.

I just didn’t consciously separate them from the enjoyment of playing.

And it’s not something confined to childhood.

I think back to wakeboarding in Hong Kong. I definitely wanted to learn how to jump the wake. There was a performance objective, and there was enormous satisfaction when I managed it.

But I don’t think my enjoyment of wakeboarding depended on whether I successfully jumped the wake that day.

I loved being out on the water. I loved experimenting, discovering what I could do and experiencing the sensation of wakeboarding, wakeskating or wake surfing itself.

The challenge was part of the enjoyment rather than the enjoyment being entirely dependent on overcoming the challenge.

I feel something similar when I’m snowboarding.

The mountains, the snow, the movement, the sensation of turning and responding to the terrain. There are moments when I’m completely absorbed in the experience.

I’m performing, learning and enjoying myself, but I’m not consciously thinking about any of those things individually.

And I certainly don’t spend much time whilst snowboarding questioning why I’m snowboarding.

It makes me wonder why golf, another activity I’ve chosen to do for pleasure, can sometimes feel so different.

Why can one recreational activity feel like play whilst another can feel almost like an examination?

Same person. Different experiences.

Perhaps what I’m exploring isn’t something new that I need to acquire. Perhaps it’s something I’ve experienced throughout my life but haven’t really considered or understood.

Trying to be, and simply being

There are other aspects of my life where I recognise something similar.

Sometimes I think about how I can be the best dad or the best husband I can be. I reflect on things I’ve said or done, and consider how I might do things differently.

But there are also countless occasions when I’m not thinking about being a good dad or a good husband at all.

I just am.

I’m enjoying a conversation, laughing at something ridiculous, sharing an experience, helping with something or learning something new about someone I love.

I’m not consciously assessing my performance. I’m not necessarily trying to learn anything. And I’m not making a deliberate effort to enjoy myself.

Yet all three can be happening quite naturally.

And I wonder whether some of the occasions when I’m at my best as a dad or a husband are precisely those occasions when I’m not thinking about how to be one.

That’s not to say that reflecting on how I can be a better husband or father isn’t worthwhile. Of course it is.

But perhaps there’s a time for thinking about the person I want to be and a time for simply being that person.

I find that distinction quite fascinating.

Has everything become about the benefit?

I’ve noticed how often I consider doing something based on the benefit it might bring me.

Exercise is good for my health. Reading is good for my mind. Stretching is good for my flexibility. Sleeping well is good for almost everything.

All perfectly sensible reasons to do those things.

But have I become a little too accustomed to justifying how I spend my time by the anticipated benefits?

What about the experience of doing something?

And what about the things I might discover along the way that I hadn’t anticipated?

When I used to practise Kung Fu, one of the aspects I particularly enjoyed was the quieter, more meditative side of it.

Yet it’s not something I naturally make time for anymore.

Why is that?

Have my interests simply changed? Or have I become more inclined to spend my time doing things where the benefit is tangible or measurable?

I don’t know.

But I wonder whether, as we get older, we become increasingly accustomed to needing a reason to do something beyond simply wanting to experience it.

As children, we play. As adults, we seem to feel the need to explain why playing is good for us.

Even our hobbies can become projects. Something to improve at, something to measure, something to achieve.

And again, I actually enjoy projects. I enjoy making things, solving problems and seeing ideas become reality.

I’m not suggesting I want to do less of those things.

I’m just curious about the difference between doing something because I enjoy it and doing something because I believe I’ll enjoy having done it.

Being where I am

Another thing I’ve started thinking about is how much time we spend mentally somewhere other than where we physically are.

Thinking about what we need to do tomorrow. Replaying a conversation from yesterday. Planning the next holiday whilst still on holiday. Worrying about what the future holds for my family. Thinking about the next shot before we’ve finished playing the current one.

Even when we’re doing something we’ve actively chosen to do, something we’ve been looking forward to, our minds can be somewhere else entirely.

It’s quite strange when you think about it.

We spend so much of our lives working towards experiences that we hope will make us happy, yet when those experiences arrive, we’re not necessarily fully there to enjoy them.

How many ordinary moments of life might actually be quite extraordinary if we were paying enough attention?

Listening to music. Walking outside. Having dinner together. Sitting by the fire. Feeling the cold mountain air on a ski lift. Laughing about something completely ridiculous with the family.

Some of my favourite experiences in life haven’t involved achieving anything particularly significant.

And yet I wouldn’t describe them as time spent achieving nothing.

They are, in many respects, the very things that make life so enjoyable.

It makes me wonder how much of my life I’m actually experiencing and how much of it I’m evaluating.

The art of simply being

Initially, reading this book made me curious about meditation and whether it might help me understand the relationship between Self 1 and Self 2.

But even as I started thinking about meditation, I noticed a rather amusing contradiction.

Am I interested in meditation because I think it will make me better at accessing Self 2?

Am I hoping it will improve my golf, help me sleep better, make me more productive or make me a better person?

Is Self 1 already developing a strategy to get more out of Self 2?

Quite possibly!

Will I actually enjoy meditation? I don’t know.

And the more I think about it, the less I’m convinced that what I’m looking for is necessarily meditation in the traditional sense.

I remember enjoying the meditative aspects of Kung Fu, but I also recognise something similar in snowboarding, wakeboarding, playing music and spending time with people I love.

I can experience it when I’m completely absorbed in solving an interesting problem or creating something new.

Perhaps I’m more interested in exploring what it means to be present in everyday life.

To experience things without immediately judging them. To enjoy things without needing to justify them. To learn things without having predetermined what I want to learn.

And to trust myself a little more in those situations where consciously trying harder might actually be getting in the way.

I don’t think being present necessarily means being still or not thinking. When I’m snowboarding, I’m anything but still. And when I’m absorbed in an interesting problem, I’m doing an enormous amount of thinking.

But there seems to be a difference between thinking about something and constantly evaluating myself whilst doing it.

And even as I write this, I wonder whether I’m turning simply being into another personal objective!

Perhaps I don’t need to become better at being. Perhaps I just need to notice when I already am.

Even writing this musing

Even in writing this musing, I find myself experiencing the very thing I’m writing about.

Am I writing because I’m enjoying exploring these thoughts? Because I’m learning something about myself? Because I want to share something interesting with my family? Or because I want them to think I’m particularly thoughtful?

And if nobody ever read this, would I still enjoy writing it?

Perhaps the answer is more interesting than the musing itself.

My initial motivation for writing this was to share some thoughts with my children.

I’ve been exploring these ideas in conversation with ChatGPT, and what’s been particularly interesting is how each exchange has prompted another thought, another memory or another question.

It’s made me think about things I hadn’t considered when I finished the book.

And I’ve realised that even if none of my children ever reads this, I’m glad I’m writing it.

I’m genuinely enjoying the experience.

The original objective hasn’t changed, but it no longer determines the value of the experience.

Perhaps I’m already experiencing the balance between performance, learning and enjoyment that I’ve been writing about.

Although, rather amusingly, having spent much of this evening reflecting on the importance of not being overly focused on outcomes, I do still want to finish this bloody musing!

I could interpret that as Self 1 interfering again. But perhaps I simply enjoy the satisfaction of completing something I’ve enjoyed creating.

Maybe the distinction isn’t whether I want to finish it, but whether I need to finish it for the experience to have been worthwhile.

Wanting an outcome is quite different from needing that outcome to validate the experience.

Finding a balance

I don’t think the answer to any of this is to stop caring about performance.

I love the satisfaction of achieving something I’ve worked hard for. I love discovering that I can do something today that I couldn’t do yesterday. I love solving problems, creating things and seeing ideas become reality.

Performance can be enormously enjoyable in itself.

Equally, learning can be enjoyable without necessarily leading to better performance. And enjoyment can be worthwhile without necessarily leading to either.

Perhaps what I’m really interested in is the relationship between the three.

When I’m too focused on performance, I might miss opportunities to learn and enjoy myself.

When I’m curious and enjoying what I’m doing, I might naturally perform better.

And when all three are happening together, perhaps that’s when I’m most absorbed in what I’m doing and least conscious of myself doing it.

I wonder whether some of the most satisfying experiences in my life have been precisely those moments.

Not because I’ve deliberately achieved a perfect balance, but because I’ve been sufficiently immersed in something for that balance to occur naturally.

And perhaps there doesn’t need to be a perfect balance in everything I do anyway.

There will be times when performance is particularly important. There will be times when I’m primarily interested in learning. And there will be times when I simply want to enjoy myself.

I’m curious about whether I recognise those differences and whether I’m consciously choosing them.

Or whether Self 1 is making those choices for me without me really noticing.

The inner game of life

I haven’t worked out a philosophy for life as a result of reading a book about golf. Far from it.

But it has made me curious about why I do the things I do, how I experience them and how much of that experience is influenced by the voice inside my head.

It’s also made me reflect on experiences I’ve had throughout my life where I’ve naturally found myself in a state that I’m now trying to understand.

Perhaps there are things worth doing simply because I’m curious about them, without needing to establish their value beforehand.

Perhaps there are occasions when I need to think more carefully and others when I need to trust myself a little more.

And perhaps there are occasions when performance, learning and enjoyment are already beautifully intertwined in my life, without me needing to do anything about it.

I don’t necessarily want to manufacture more of those moments. But I’d quite like to recognise and appreciate them when they happen.

I’m sure I’ll have plenty more thoughts on all of this. And I’m equally sure that the next time I’m standing over a golf ball, I’ll still have a ridiculous number of thoughts going through my head.

But perhaps I’ll notice them a little differently.

It’s funny that I picked up a book hoping to take a few shots off my golf handicap and ended up questioning why I wanted to take a few shots off my golf handicap in the first place.

And now, having finally finished this musing, I find myself experiencing the satisfaction of having completed something, the pleasure of having written it and the enjoyment of everything I’ve discovered along the way.

Performance, learning and enjoyment.

Perhaps that’s enough for tonight.
